# Civic Engagement Methods and Sustained Turnout

What does the literature say about which interventions actually move people from "occasional voter" to "sustained participant" - not just once, but repeatedly? This matters for UVote because the product bets that a low-friction, recurring civic action (voting on bills between elections) can build a participation habit that carries over into formal elections.

---

## Social pressure / social comparison increases turnout more than persuasion appeals

**Citation:** Gerber, A. S., Green, D. P., & Larimer, C. W. (2008). "Social Pressure and Voter Turnout: Evidence from a Large-Scale Field Experiment." *American Political Science Review*, 102(1), 33-48. https://doi.org/10.1017/S000305540808009X

**Summary:** In a field experiment with roughly 180,000 Michigan voters, mailers that showed recipients their own and their neighbors' past turnout, and promised to publicize their turnout after the election, raised turnout by 8.1 percentage points relative to a control group - substantially larger than any standard GOTV messaging effect. The effect came from social visibility and comparison to a reference group (household/neighborhood), not from information about candidates or issues.

**How this supports UVote:** This is direct evidence for the value of the **District Score** (citizen vs. their own community) as an engagement mechanic, not just a reporting feature. Showing a user how their vote compares to their neighbors' aggregated votes - visibly, repeatedly, per bill - is the same lever this experiment found to be one of the most powerful known turnout interventions. It argues for surfacing district-level comparison prominently on the vote screen itself, not burying it in a separate stats page.

---

## Emphasizing high (not low) turnout increases the motivation to participate

**Citation:** Gerber, A. S., & Rogers, T. (2009). "Descriptive Social Norms and Motivation to Vote: Everybody's Voting and So Should You." *The Journal of Politics*, 71(1), 178-191. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381608090117

**Summary:** Two GOTV field experiments found that messages emphasizing high expected turnout ("most people are voting") increased intention to vote more than messages emphasizing low turnout or declining participation, especially among infrequent and occasional voters. Messages that lament low participation appear to backfire by signaling that non-participation is the norm.

**How this supports UVote:** This is a direct, specific UX-copy argument: any UI element that shows participation stats ("X% of your district has voted on this bill," aggregate vote counts, follower counts on representatives) should be framed around participation levels that are already high or rising, never around how few people have voted. It also cautions against messaging like "only 12% of your district has weighed in" on low-traffic bills - the literature suggests this could suppress rather than encourage voting.

## Reducing the friction of the "how/when" of voting increases turnout independent of motivation

**Citation:** Nickerson, D. W., & Rogers, T. (2010). "Do You Have a Voting Plan? Implementation Intentions, Voter Turnout, and Organic Plan Making." *Psychological Science*, 21(2), 194-199. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609359326

**Summary:** In a field experiment with over 287,000 voters during the 2008 election, calls that helped people form a concrete plan (what time, from where, doing what beforehand) raised turnout by 4.1 percentage points overall and 9.1 points among single-voter households - while a standard GOTV script with no planning prompt had no significant effect. The mechanism is procedural friction reduction, not persuasion.

**How this supports UVote:** Supports investing in low-friction mechanics that turn intent into completed action in the moment: pre-filled address/district lookup, one-tap up/down voting directly on the bill card, and notification nudges tied to a specific upcoming vote deadline rather than generic "come vote" reminders. This is evidence that UX friction, not civic apathy alone, is a primary lever - directly relevant to prioritizing address autofill and single-screen voting flows over adding more informational content.

## Hyperlocal participatory engagement measurably increases subsequent formal voting - especially among low-propensity voters

**Citation:** Johnson, C., Carlson, H. J., & Reynolds, S. (2021). "Testing the Participation Hypothesis: Evidence from Participatory Budgeting." *Political Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-021-09679-w

**Summary:** By linking New York City participatory budgeting (PB) participants to official voter files, the authors found that participating in PB increased an individual's probability of voting in subsequent regular elections by an average of 8.4 percentage points, with larger effects among groups that typically vote at lower rates (young people, lower-income, less-educated, and Black voters). This is one of the few studies to causally link a *local, non-electoral* civic action to *later formal electoral turnout*.

**How this supports UVote:** This is the strongest available evidence for UVote's core growth thesis: that a low-effort, recurring local civic action (voting on bills) can function as an on-ramp that increases formal voter turnout later, rather than merely substituting for it. It supports pitching UVote's impact not only as "more informed constituents" but as a documented turnout-multiplier, and supports prioritizing outreach in lower-turnout demographics rather than optimizing solely for already-high-propensity users.
