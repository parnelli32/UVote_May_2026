# Case Study: Countable

## Founders

Countable was founded by **Bart Myers** (CEO) and **Peter Arzhintar**, who had previously cofounded SideReel, a web-based television-tracking company they sold to TiVo in 2011. Myers had earlier been involved with Howard Dean's 2004 presidential campaign and set out to build a company focused on politics — initially around campaign finance reform and online privacy legislation.
- **Source:** [InfluenceWatch: Countable Corporation](https://www.influencewatch.org/for-profit/countable-corporation/); [Wikipedia: Countable (company)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_(company))

**Founding date discrepancy, flagged rather than smoothed over:** Wikipedia and InfluenceWatch state Countable was founded in **2013**. A TechCrunch article directly quoting CEO Bart Myers (Oct 20, 2021) instead states the company "has raised about $16 million since the company was founded in **2014**." Both sources were fetched and verified directly; they simply disagree by one year. Not resolved further in this pass.

**Where the founders are today:**
- **Bart Myers** stepped back from day-to-day leadership of Countable in **February 2024**, becoming **Chairman of Countable Corp.**, and in **April 2025** was announced as the new **CEO of Crowdpac** (a separate political-fundraising/engagement platform, unrelated to Countable's post-acquisition owner) with a fresh $4 million funding round. His own words on taking the Crowdpac role: *"Our country needs a trusted platform to bring Americans together and strengthen our democracy... As one of the first and the leading platform of its kind, Crowdpac has a strong foundation upon which to build upon so we can meet this moment—and what's coming next."*
  - **Source:** [PR Newswire, April 22, 2025](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/crowdpac-announces-bart-myers-as-ceo-and-a-new-advisory-board-to-leverage-a-successful-4m-funding-round-302434648.html)
- No current, verified information on Peter Arzhintar's present role was found in this research pass.

## Funding

- Canaan Partners was involved as an investing partner from Countable's founding.
- **Series A: $12 million**, announced **October 20, 2021**, led by existing investor Canaan Partners, joined by Ulysses Management and Global Catalyst Partners — raised explicitly "for virtual brand communities," i.e., after the pivot to enterprise engagement was already underway.
- **Total raised: approximately $16 million** since founding, per Bart Myers directly quoted by TechCrunch.
- No valuation was ever disclosed in any source found.
- **Source:** [TechCrunch, "Countable brings in $12M for virtual brand communities," Oct 20, 2021](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/countable-brings-in-12m-for-virtual-brand-communities/)

## What happened / the pivot: full timeline

1. **2013 (or 2014 — see discrepancy above): Founded** as a consumer civic-engagement app. Countable.us let users track upcoming Congressional legislation by topic (e.g., DACA, Poverty, Human Trafficking), read plain-language summaries, contact their representatives with one click, and vote support/oppose on bills — votes and messages were aggregated and surfaced back to congressional offices.
2. **May 1, 2019: Acquired Brigade and Causes.** Causes.com was one of the first 10 Facebook platform apps, founded by Facebook co-founder Sean Parker, and had grown to "a jaw-dropping 200 million users" at its peak. Parker later sold Causes into his newer civic-tech effort Brigade, which raised a $9.3M Series A under Parker's backing but never achieved the scale it needed. Brigade's engineering team was acqui-hired by Pinterest; its technology, data, and open-sourced voter-matching software went to Countable.
3. **2020: Consumer product merges away.** Countable.us merged into Causes.com (Countable.us now redirects there). Simultaneously, **Countable.com** (formerly "Countable Pro") relaunched as the company's **enterprise B2B arm**, providing community/engagement tooling to corporations, non-profits, and political campaigns — early named customers include Starbucks, Uber, Levi's, Patagonia, and Bernie Sanders' campaign. In August 2020, Countable's technology powered the **virtual Democratic National Convention**.
4. **October 2021: $12M Series A** for the now fully-enterprise "virtual brand communities" product. Per Myers, revenue grew nearly 300% and annual recurring revenue grew 277% in the prior year; the company had made over 120 brand "implementations."
5. **June 6, 2024: Acquired by EV3 Global**, a company focused on "employment intelligence and AI." Countable — by then based in Oakland, CA — became part of EV3's employee-engagement product suite, explicitly repositioned around "the trillion-dollar employee turnover problem," i.e., internal corporate HR/engagement, not civic engagement in any form.
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia: Countable (company)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_(company)); [TechCrunch, "Sean Parker's Brigade/Causes acquired by govtech app Countable," May 1, 2019](https://techcrunch.com/2019/05/01/brigade-countable/); [TechCrunch, Oct 20, 2021](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/countable-brings-in-12m-for-virtual-brand-communities/); [Reworked.co / EV3 Global acquisition coverage, June 2024](https://www.reworked.co/the-wire/ev3-global-successfully-acquires-countable-enhancing-employee-engagement-capabilities/)

## Meaningful quotes

On the original mission (Bart Myers, quoted in a hands-on 2014 product review):
> "We found that there was a huge disconnect between politics and people, with few resources for the average citizen to keep up with what's going on in Congress and what kind of legislation is being passed. The majority of Americans are generally dissatisfied with the job their legislators are doing and we thought, wouldn't it be great to create a way for people to know what's going on and give them a way to actually make a difference?"
— Source: [Social Media Today, "Social Advocacy & Politics: An Advocacy Tool Review of Countable," Aug 12, 2014](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/content/social-advocacy-politics-advocacy-tool-review-countable)

On why the original one-click model would appeal to young users (same source, same date):
> "by summarizing legislation with succinct yea and nay arguments, we're essentially translating congress speak into everyday language. Similarly, by offering one click communication, letting your congressperson know how you feel about issues is as easy as 'liking' a post on Facebook. These features especially appeal to Millennials, whom are often underrepresented in the political landscape."

On absorbing Brigade's mission (Bart Myers, TechCrunch, May 1, 2019):
> "Brigade was founded with the non-partisan mission to reinvent how Americans participate in politics. When they decided to bring their journey to a close, Matt and Brigade's leadership team sought a mission-aligned company to acquire their technology, and a responsible place to point any members of their community who were eager to remain civically active and engaged. They approached Countable — an obvious fit for our commitment to lowering barriers to civic entry and empowering meaningful action, and we're excited to provide a home for their technology moving forward."
(The same article notes Countable's civic tooling had by then "powered 35 million civic actions.")

On Brigade's own failure, from its CEO Matt Mahan (TechCrunch, May 1, 2019) — directly relevant as this is civic-tech IP Countable itself absorbed:
> "While we didn't reach the ultimate mountaintop, I think we moved the entire civic tech space forward."
> "By removing too many barriers, debates empowered the loudest and most aggressive voices in the room." (on Brigade's 2016 pivot to open "debates," which backfired)
> "after two election cycles Brigade had not achieved the user scale we know is required to fundamentally transform our politics . . . For a company set up to be a civic moonshot, this was simply not good enough."

TechCrunch's own editorial framing of the broader pattern (Josh Constine, May 1, 2019):
> "Consumer-focused apps for improving democracy are tough to monetize."

On the actual reason for Countable's pivot to enterprise (Bart Myers, TechCrunch, Oct 20, 2021):
> "A lot of things are changing quickly with social media, which has become more toxic in some ways and less trustful about safety and security of its users, as well as taking away some of the controls that brands had to engage with their audience on their terms. It used to be that you had free viral reach and traffic, but that has gone away, and it is now pay-for-play, which is not so good for reaching people organically."
> "[We're doing for community engagement] like what Shopify did with the shopping cart."

## Biggest flaws

1. **Documented, contemporary, hands-on product criticism (2014):** the Social Media Today review above is the strongest first-hand critique found. Key excerpts:
   > "The app has potential, but is still a little rough. It is fairly exhaustive, displaying ALL the upcoming bill votes in Congress. That means the interface is a bit cluttered with some really unimportant bills and bills offered simply for political posturing that will have no impact on reality."
   > "the descriptions of the bills on Countable are problematic. Countable staff manually writes them, which can be a daunting task, to say the least. Keeping the descriptions neutral is difficult, at best, and the temptation to spin them one way or the other must be strong."
   > "there is insufficient information for people to make informed choices. Many of the descriptions just don't tell me enough. In most cases, I really wanted a link to the full text of the bill to make my judgment, but that was not available."
   > "the weakness of many of the descriptions are especially problematic because Countable is asking people to vote up or down on each bill before they even see the information available on the app, let alone the full text. I am all for getting people engaged in legislative politics, but I am also a big fan of an informed public. And this app still encourages people to make [un]informed decisions."
2. **Business-model flaw, evidenced by the company's own trajectory:** the free consumer product never became a durable, monetizable business on its own. Myers's own account of why the company pivoted (2021 quote above) is that organic/viral social distribution dried up — the free-to-reach-citizens model depended on cheap distribution that stopped being cheap. The company only found a defensible paying customer once it retargeted the same underlying engagement technology at **brands and institutions willing to pay directly**, not at citizens.
3. **Category-level flaw, evidenced by Brigade's failure** (the civic-tech company Countable's own IP absorbed): even a Sean Parker-backed, well-funded consumer democracy app (Brigade, $9.3M Series A) failed to reach "the user scale [it knew was] required to fundamentally transform our politics" after two full election cycles — and TechCrunch's own framing of this event was that "consumer-focused apps for improving democracy are tough to monetize" as a category, not a Brigade-specific failure.

## Comparison to UVote

| Trait | Original Countable (2013/14–2020) | UVote |
|---|---|---|
| Address-to-rep matching | Not confirmed in any source found — Countable let users follow topics and representatives, but no evidence of automatic address-based district resolution as an onboarding step | Yes, hard-gated at signup via Philadelphia's AIS API |
| Multi-body live bills | No — Congress only, for the entirety of its consumer-product life | Yes (planned): City Council + PA House + PA Senate simultaneously |
| Citizen votes on a bill | **Yes** — this is the closest match to UVote's own core mechanic found anywhere in this entire research effort (both this case study and `competitive-landscape.md`) | Yes |
| Citizen-vs-rep comparison / standing score | No — sentiment was aggregated and surfaced to congressional offices, but never published as a persistent, checkable alignment score | Yes — the Constituent/District/Representation score triad is the platform's core mechanic |

**What UVote does differently that might avoid the same failure — stated plainly, without softening:**

- **The documented informed-consent flaw is directly, explicitly addressed by an existing UVote design principle.** The 2014 review's core complaint — that Countable let people vote before they'd seen adequate information, with staff-written summaries of uncertain neutrality — is precisely what UVote's stated "No vote without context" principle (`CLAUDE.md`) is built to prevent: a mandatory plain-language, Grade-8 summary is required before any vote is allowed. This is a real, structural difference, not just a stated intention — it's evidence a documented predecessor failure mode is already designed against.
- **UVote's monetization does not depend on discovering a payer after the fact.** Countable spent roughly seven years as a free-to-citizens consumer product before finding a business model (paying brands/institutions), and only found it by abandoning the citizen product almost entirely. UVote's business model (`docs/business-model.md`) puts the paying customer — the elected official's office — into the plan from day one, tied directly to the platform's own core accountability mechanic (the Constituent score and divergence-explanation prompt), not bolted on as an unrelated enterprise pivot years later.
- **Where this comparison should NOT be softened:** UVote's official/B2B tier is, structurally, the same kind of answer Countable was eventually forced into — a paying institutional customer funding a product citizens use for free. The honest reading of Countable's history is not "UVote avoids Countable's failure mode" so much as "UVote is choosing, deliberately and from the start, the same kind of two-sided answer Countable stumbled into only after its original consumer-only model didn't work." That should be treated as **validated market signal for the model UVote already chose**, not evidence UVote is immune to the underlying problem: consumer-only civic engagement, unsupported by an institutional payer, has now failed to sustain itself at least twice in this research (Countable's original product, and Brigade before it). UVote's B2B/official tier isn't a hedge against that risk — it's a direct acknowledgment that the risk is real and needs a paying side from day one.
